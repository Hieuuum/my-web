From a young age till university, there was a question that I always dodged and didn't give much thought until recently: "What do you want to do when you grow up?"

When I was young, I was always mesmerized by Masterchef TV shows. It was very fun to watch people cook under pressure to deliver a satisfying dish with good taste, execution, and appearance. Even if the dish looked perfect in my eyes, the judges would always find small things to critique and eliminate the participants. There were also rare moments when the dishes tied, resulting in no elimination. A few months ago, I also watched Culinary Class Wars, a popular cooking competition show on Netflix. Maybe that's one of the reasons why I appreciate food so much and wanted to become a chef. Wouldn't it be cool if you could cook yourself delicious meals every day?

I also wanted to become an astronaut. The farthest object humans have set foot on is the Moon, which is 406,771 km (or 252,756 miles) away from Earth. Even such a long distance is infinitesimally small compared to the vastness of space. The furthest object that we've discovered is MoM-z14, about 33.8 billion light-years away. That's 3.198 × 10²³ km (or 1.987 × 10²³ miles), which is 8.319 × 10¹⁷ times the Earth-Moon distance.

![Photo of starry sky](/images/posts/why-i-chose-ai-safety/img_4386.jpeg "A picture of the clear and dark night sky I took at Death Valley National Park.")

Even now, both of these jobs still sound cool to me. However, as I grew up, I didn't want to pursue them anymore. A chef's median pay is low, while astronauts go on dangerous missions. 

In September of 2024, I arrived in the US. During that time, I wanted to become a Software Engineer instead. I thought the pay was high. I thought you could do interesting work because to get a Software Engineering role, you need to do LeetCode problems during interviews, which requires a good understanding of data structures and communication skills.

However, I also realized this isn't sustainable. Even if I landed a SWE role, I might not work on a meaningful cause with high impact. And I also didn't feel that motivated studying for Software Engineering except for the money, which isn't a good thing to pursue.

During sophomore year, AI safety caught my interest. I don't remember exactly when, but I vaguely remember hearing that the leading AI scientists saying that there is a very high chance of human extinction within the next 5-10 years. It went under the radar until my friend Avi told me that he also wanted to work in the field. 

I started doing more research, and became more convinced that AI safety deserves more attention. A neuron in a human's brain sends information through electric signals once its combined input exceeds a certain threshold. Neurons in Deep Learning try to mimic that: their output is just a linear combination of inputs, all of which are numbers. Current AI systems have billions, or even trillions, of neurons. Intelligence in these systems emerges from their training process; we didn't write every single rule for their behavior. They are "grown", not "built". And the sad part is that we have a very poor understanding of them. We cannot predict how these systems behave under every situation. In the future, intelligent AI systems that are misaligned with human values or misused might cause catastrophic events. 

Because of that, I decided to work in AI safety.
